The question
If a British electricity supplier moved into Spain, would the same rooftop product work — and what breaks first? The answer is not sunshine. It is roof per household, the value of a self-consumed kilowatt-hour, and the owner vote needed to use a common roof.

What you are looking at

Every roof here was run through the same chain as the single block in this study: NOAA sun position, Ineichen–Perez clear sky, day-type clearness, Erbs split, Hay–Davies transposition, a geometric horizon built from the real neighbouring footprints, Faiman cell temperature. No kWh/kWp lookup anywhere.

Shading is computed against every building in the extract, including the ones that carry no panels — a church or an office still casts a shadow. That is why they are drawn.

Heights are mostly inferred from typology rather than measured. Switch the colour mode to height source to see which roofs are which.

What the model found
More sun does not mean more value: a Spanish avoided kilowatt-hour is worth about half a British one. A collective rooftop system can pay back because fixed costs are shared; a one-flat installation cannot. Batteries improve self-consumption but do not pay for themselves on the retail spread. The deployment constraint is a junta de propietarios vote, so this is an operations problem as much as an engineering one.

Caveat

This is a case study, not a survey. The Ensanche typology the model was calibrated on does not hold across every district here, dwelling counts are derived rather than counted, and roof obstructions are a flat haircut. Read the numbers as ranges.

Madrid has 1.8× Britain's sunshine and a fifth of the value per household.

This is a case study, not a proposal. I built a rooftop photovoltaic model for Madrid from the sun's position upward, to find out whether a rooftop product designed for British houses works in a Spanish apartment city. It does not, and the reason has almost nothing to do with sunlight.

The model computes solar position from NOAA/Meeus, clear-sky irradiance from Ineichen–Perez, the beam/diffuse split from Erbs, and plane-of-array irradiance from Hay–Davies. It casts shadows geometrically from real building footprints, panel by panel, hour by hour, and prices output under the Spanish 2.0TD tariff and Real Decreto 244/2019. Every input lives in config/madrid.yaml with a source and confidence label.

Economics, not just generation

A panel only earns its avoided retail price when somebody is using the electricity. In the model, self-consumed energy is valued at the demand-weighted Spanish 2.0TD price of €0.133/kWh; exported energy is valued at €0.05/kWh and is capped monthly by imported energy. The live building card applies a transparent 70% self-consumption / 30% export split to each selected building and reports gross energy value, not accounting profit.

Seven findings

0.97kWp per dwelling

Roof area per household, not sunshine, is the Spanish constraint

93 addressable roofs on the block carry 2,492 kWp across an estimated 2,580 dwellings—about 1,600 kWh a year each. A British semi-detached house has roughly 4 kWp. Six floors sharing one roof cancels most of Iberia's solar advantage.

€0.133vs €0.305 in GB

A Spanish kilowatt-hour avoided is worth about half a British one

44% of household demand falls in the cheap valle band at €0.093/kWh, giving a demand-weighted avoided price of €0.133. The GB unit rate is 26.11p, about €0.305. One household's rooftop is worth roughly €213 a year in Madrid against €1,160 in Britain.

2.7 : 1kept vs exported

The economics turn on self-consumption, not irradiance

A self-consumed kWh avoids the full retail price; an exported one earns about €0.05, capped monthly at the value imported, with surplus forfeited. The household load curve matters more than the exact sun curve: dinner is at 21:30, not 19:00.

4.3 yrvs never, alone

The product is a signed resolution from a comunidad de propietarios

One flat fitting three panels pays €4.16/Wp and never pays back because €4,800 of inverter, scaffold, electrical board and legalisation does not shrink. The collective installation pays €0.64/Wp, €908 per household, back in 4.3 years. Same hardware, same sun; the difference is who signed.

25 yrstorage payback

A domestic battery is a poor household investment and a good trading asset

Storage lifts self-consumption from 46% to 99% and earns about €12,200 a year more. It costs €307,000: a 25-year payback. To reach eight years it needs another €26,000 a year from balancing, capacity or wholesale spreads.

0%of 800 W cap used

The plug-in kit routes around the constraint

A vertical south-facing panel in a 20 m street yields 382 kWh—49% less than the roof. Two make 765 kWh/year and the 800 W inverter cap clips exactly nothing. A €620 kit pays back in 7.4 years with no roof rights, junta vote, scaffold, installer or electrical certificate.

2.3%all urban shading

Dense-city shading barely matters here

68% of the PVGIS gap is the deliberate flat tilt that fits 67% more panels; 26% is neighbouring buildings, 5% row spacing and 0.5% parapet. All urban obstruction costs 2.3%, not the 15–25% intuition suggests. The uniform cornice line keeps roofs above neighbours' shadows; the streets are dark all winter and roofs are in full sun.

At the December solstice, 16:30, the original 5,538-panel block view found 5,493 panels still in direct sun: streets can be dark while roofs remain productive. Shading bites where the cornice line breaks, especially around post-1960 towers on the Castellana axis.

Height provenance is visible, not hidden. In the original block extract, six of 100 buildings carried an OSM floor count and the other 94 had heights inferred from typology. The city extract improves this to 33.5% measured or floor-count provenance, but heights remain the clearest data limitation.

The constraint

Not irradiance, module cost or grid connection. The roof is a common element under the Ley de Propiedad Horizontal. Selling means winning a junta de propietarios vote—one third of owners and one third of ownership quotas—on roughly a 40-day meeting cycle. 28% of households are tenants who cannot authorise it. Deployment is therefore set by the sales cycle, so the first measures are time to signed resolution, junta conversion and internal champions.

Two results I did not expect

The per-panel tilt optimum is not the per-roof optimum

At 37° a panel earns about 4% more than at 15° but needs 2.4× the row pitch, so half as many fit. The roof is scarce, not the module.

Below 10° rain stops clearing glass and the constant 2.5% soiling loss would roughly double in summer. 15° is practical; the binding reason is maintenance, not physics. Worst-to-best panel spans 26%, sky-view factor 0.77–0.98, and marginal cost is flat at €277 while average cost runs from €11.24/Wp for one panel to €0.95/Wp at 32.

Where I was wrong

  1. Diffuse irradiance: a monthly mean fed into a convex Erbs correlation overstated diffuse fraction at 0.41 versus about 0.31 and pushed POA 7.5% below PVGIS. Day-type mixtures fixed it and gave the battery genuine consecutive overcast days.
  2. East and west: a negation swapped them. Totals barely moved, but morning obstructions were on the wrong side. A direct 08:00 test caught it.
  3. Wrong denominator: a whole block roof was measured against one household, collapsing self-consumption to 8%. Collective self-consumption is the correct frame.
  4. Withdrawn claim: pooling 118 households raised self-consumption only 44% → 46%; diversity smooths the evening peak, not the midday solar surplus. The gain is cost amortisation.
  5. €620 balcony kit: rooftop maintenance and inverter replacement were incorrectly charged to it, turning a 7.4-year payback into 26 years.
  6. Undensified edges: sampling building outlines only at their vertices let sunlight through the middle of long facades, inflating winter yield on courtyard roofs. Densifying every edge fixed the geometry.

Every input at the top is commercial or regulatory, not physical. The physics is the easy half.

Named failure modes: the load curve is synthetic and self-consumption is the most valuable output; dwellings per building are derived rather than counted; 94% of heights are inferred; shading is binary per panel rather than per cell string; the rooftop obstruction share of 22% was eyeballed from aerial imagery on one block; tariff bands are assumed to survive thirty years; July clearness is 0.98 of clear sky; and one Ensanche block cannot represent Vallecas or the post-war periphery.

One question I could not settle

Whether a plug-in kit under 800 W is exempt from Spanish self-consumption formalities is genuinely disputed in the sources. Retail and comparison material widely states that installations up to 800 W of inverter output follow a simplified route with no technical project and no certified installer, on the German VDE-AR-N 4105 model. The more careful reading is that RD 244/2019 creates no such exemption: the 800 VA threshold appears in ITC-BT-40 as an independent-circuit requirement in certain no-export cases, not as a permit exemption.

Whether the community must authorise it depends on whether the balcony railing is private or common under the title deeds. This is a real commercial risk to a product whose advantage is avoiding paperwork; one call to a Spanish energy lawyer should close it.

Prior art

A 2025 Bocconi Students for Machine Learning project by Bryan Bisetti, Andrea Duico and Filip Juren trained a U-Net to segment rooftops for PV suitability, training on Ljubljana and testing on Milan. It reported about 95% training accuracy and 85% IoU, against sub-80% validation accuracy and roughly 50% validation IoU, failing on sloped roofs. Its stated next step was azimuth and 3D geometry.

A rooftop mask tells you area; it does not tell you tilt, azimuth or what the building opposite is doing at four o'clock in December. This model starts with OSM footprints and spends effort on geometry and economics instead; segmentation would earn its place generating footprints and obstruction maps at city scale.
