Madrid has 1.8× Britain's sunshine and a fifth of the value per household.

This is a case study, not a proposal. I built a rooftop photovoltaic model for one real Madrid block from the sun's position upward, to find out whether a rooftop product designed for British houses works in a Spanish apartment city. It does not, and the reason has almost nothing to do with sunlight.

The model computes solar position from the NOAA/Meeus algorithm, clear-sky irradiance from Ineichen-Perez, the beam/diffuse split from Erbs, and plane-of-array irradiance from Hay-Davies. It then casts shadows geometrically from 100 real building footprints, panel by panel, hour by hour, and prices the output under the Spanish 2.0TD tariff and the self-consumption rules in Real Decreto 244/2019. Everything is in config/madrid.yaml with a source and a confidence label.

Calibration first

The model is fitted on twelve numbers — monthly global horizontal irradiance for Madrid — and then checked against two figures it never saw. Calibrating on one quantity and validating on another is the only way to tell whether the physics in between is right or whether the fit is quietly absorbing the error.

Six findings

0.97kWp per dwelling

Roof area per household, not sunshine, is the Spanish constraint

93 addressable roofs on this block carry 2,492 kWp across an estimated 2,580 dwellings — about 1,600 kWh a year each. A British semi-detached house has a whole roof to itself, roughly 4 kWp. Six floors sharing one roof cancels most of Iberia's solar advantage before a euro is spent.

€0.133vs €0.305 in GB

A Spanish kilowatt-hour avoided is worth about half a British one

Under the regulated three-period tariff, 44% of household demand falls in the cheap valle band at €0.093/kWh, so the demand-weighted avoided price is €0.133. The GB unit rate under the Ofgem cap is 26.11 p, about €0.305, and it is flat. Combine this with the finding above and one household's rooftop is worth roughly €213 a year in Madrid against €1,160 in Britain.

2.7 : 1kept vs exported

The economics turn on self-consumption, not irradiance

A self-consumed kilowatt-hour avoids the full retail price. An exported one earns about €0.05, capped monthly at the value of what you imported, with the surplus forfeited. So the model's accuracy about when the household is home matters more than its accuracy about when the sun shines — which makes the Spanish clock, dinner at 21:30 rather than 19:00, a first-order input rather than local colour.

4.3 yrvs never, alone

The product is a signed resolution from a comunidad de propietarios

One flat fitting its own share of the roof — three panels — pays €4.16/Wp and never pays back, because €4,800 of inverter, scaffold, electrical board and legalisation does not care how many panels it carries. The same roof as a collective installation across all 118 dwellings pays €0.64/Wp, €908 per household, back in 4.3 years. Same hardware, same sun. The difference is who signed.

25 yrstorage payback

A domestic battery is a poor household investment and a good trading asset

Storage lifts self-consumption from 46% to 99% and earns about €12,200 a year more on this roof. It costs €307,000 — a 25-year payback against a cycle life of roughly the same length. To reach eight years it would need to earn another €26,000 a year from somewhere other than the customer's bill: balancing, capacity, or wholesale spreads captured by an aggregator. That is the real argument for vertical integration, and it is arithmetic rather than a slogan. If it holds, the supplier should own the battery and sell the household a tariff.

0%of the 800 W cap used

The plug-in kit routes around the constraint, and the famous limit doesn't bind

A vertical south-facing panel in a 20 m Ensanche street, opposite six floors, yields 382 kWh — 49% less than on the roof. Two of them make 765 kWh a year, and the 800 W inverter cap clips exactly nothing, because a vertical panel in a street canyon never approaches its rating. A €620 kit pays back in 7.4 years and needs no roof rights, no junta vote, no scaffold, no installer and no electrical certificate. It trades half the sun for the whole of the addressable market.

2.3%all urban shading

Dense-city shading barely matters here — which cost me my own top data request

I assumed most of the gap between in-situ yield and the PVGIS headline was the city: six-storey walls, narrow streets. Taking it apart one obstruction at a time says otherwise. 68% of the gap is the flat-tilt choice I made deliberately to fit 67% more panels; 26% is neighbouring buildings; 5% is row spacing; 0.5% is the parapet. All urban obstruction together is worth 2.3% of yield, against the 15–25% that intuition suggests.

Why, and what it changes

The reason is the cornice line. The 1860–1930 grid was built to a uniform height, so every roof sits above every neighbour's shadow. At the December solstice a same-height neighbour stands only a parapet above the panel plane and so reaches about three metres — not the ninety its shadow runs at street level. The streets are dark all winter and the roofs are in full sun. Set the map above to 21 December, 16:30 and switch the colour to Lit now: the streets go black and 5,493 of 5,538 panels are still in direct sun. Shading bites where the cornice line breaks, which here means the post-1960 towers on the Castellana axis.

This cost me an argument, and it is the most useful thing the model did. I had ranked building heights as the top data request, because 94% of them are inferred and heights drive shading. If all urban obstruction is worth 2.3%, a one-storey error in the typology prior moves the answer by a fraction of a percent. So the LiDAR request drops to third, and the two inputs that actually move the answer — the household load curve and the dwelling count — are both about people rather than geometry. It also means the per-panel shading model is more machinery than this block needed. I would keep it, because the number it produced is one I can defend and because it will matter in Vallecas or Tetuán where the skyline is irregular. I would not build it first again.

The constraint

Not irradiance. Not module cost. Not grid connection at this scale. In an apartment city the roof is a common element under the Ley de Propiedad Horizontal. Selling a rooftop system means winning a vote at a junta de propietarios — one third of owners and one third of ownership quotas, on a roughly 40-day meeting cycle, with no single decision-maker to sell to. And 28% of these households are tenants who cannot authorise it at all.

The deployment rate is therefore set by the sales cycle, not by the sun. That makes it an operations problem rather than an engineering one. The three things I would measure first: time from first contact to signed resolution, conversion rate at the junta, and the share of buildings where one owner can be recruited as an internal champion.

Two results I did not expect

The per-panel tilt optimum is not the per-roof optimum

At 37° a panel earns about 4% more than at 15°. It also needs 2.4× the row pitch, so half as many fit. Per roof the flatter array wins by a wide margin, because the roof is the scarce input rather than the module.

I would still not build the 5° case, and the reason is not in the table. Below about 10° rain stops clearing the glass, so the soiling loss this model holds constant at 2.5% would roughly double in a Madrid summer and the array would need washing. 15° is the practical answer and the binding reason is maintenance, not physics.

Panel-level variance is wide enough to change decisions

Worst to best panel on this block spans 26%, and sky view factor ranges from 0.77 to 0.98. A system-level average hides the fact that the last row added is often the one that loses money. That is why the unit of analysis throughout is a single panel, and why capex is costed marginally: the marginal cost of one more panel is flat at €277, while the average runs from €11.24/Wp on a one-panel job to €0.95/Wp at thirty-two.

Where I was wrong

Five bugs, each now covered by a regression test. They are here because the corrections are the most informative part of the build.

  1. Diffuse irradiance overstated by feeding a monthly mean into a convex correlation. The first version scaled clear-sky irradiance by a monthly mean clearness index and passed that mean to the Erbs diffuse correlation. The diffuse fraction is convex in clearness, so a month at mean 0.80 is not thirty days at 0.80 — it is roughly twenty-four cloudless days and six overcast ones. The model reported an annual diffuse fraction of 0.41 against a real Madrid value near 0.31, silently converting beam into diffuse and pushing plane-of-array irradiance 7.5% below PVGIS. Fixed by representing each month as a mixture of day types, which also fixed a second problem for free: the battery model now sees genuine consecutive overcast days, which a monthly mean can never produce and which flatters storage badly.
  2. East and west were swapped. Converting solar azimuth from the compass convention to the south-zero convention is a subtraction; I wrote a negation. Annual totals barely moved, because the array azimuth is −6° and the sun's path is nearly symmetric about noon, so the error was invisible in every headline number. It was caught only by a test that checks the sign at 08:00 directly. It mattered: with east and west reversed, every shading calculation put the morning obstruction on the wrong side of the street.
  3. A whole block roof was measured against one household's demand. Wrong denominator. Self-consumption collapsed to 8% and every configuration looked uneconomic. The correct frame is collective self-consumption, where output is allocated across participating dwellings.
  4. A claim I had to withdraw. I expected pooling 118 staggered households to raise self-consumption materially, and I built a diversified load model to show it. It does not: 44% → 46%. The aggregate curve is genuinely flatter, but the solar surplus arrives at midday and almost nobody's routine moves into midday. Diversity smooths the evening peak, which is the wrong peak. So the whole gain from doing the building together is cost amortisation, not shared consumption — a different mechanism from the one I assumed, and it changes what you would sell.
  5. A €620 balcony kit was charged rooftop maintenance. The cash-flow function applied a flat annual maintenance charge plus a mid-life string-inverter replacement to everything, including a plug-in kit with nothing to service. The charge was larger than the asset. It turned a 7.4-year payback into 26 years.

Every input at the top of that table is commercial or regulatory. None is physical. That is the shape of the whole study: the physics is the easy half.

Provenance is shown, not hidden. Six of the 100 buildings in this extract carry a floor count in OpenStreetMap; the other 94 have heights inferred from typology. Turn on the height provenance layer above and the map shows exactly which roofs are measured and which are guessed. This was my largest stated weakness until the attribution above demoted it.

The named failure modes, in the order I would now attack them: the load curve is synthetic and self-consumption is the model's most valuable output; dwellings per building is derived rather than counted, and it is the denominator of the headline result; 94% of heights are inferred, which matters less here than I claimed but would matter in an irregular district; shading is binary per panel rather than per cell string, so the real electrical loss from partial shade is worse than the geometric loss; the rooftop obstruction share of 22% was eyeballed from aerial imagery on one block; the tariff band structure is assumed to survive thirty years, which it will not; July clearness sits at 0.98 of clear sky, leaving no room for a hazy summer; and one block of one typology cannot carry a city extrapolation — the Ensanche is not Vallecas or the post-war periphery.

One question I could not settle

Whether a plug-in kit under 800 W is exempt from Spanish self-consumption formalities is genuinely disputed in the sources, and I am flagging it rather than taking the convenient reading. Retail and comparison material widely states that installations up to 800 W of inverter output follow a simplified route with no technical project and no certified installer, on the German VDE-AR-N 4105 model. The more careful reading is that RD 244/2019 creates no such exemption at all: the 800 VA threshold appears in ITC-BT-40 as an independent-circuit requirement in certain no-export cases, not as a permit exemption.

Separately, whether the community of owners must authorise it depends on whether the balcony railing is a private or common element — a question about that building's title deeds, not about national law. This is a real commercial risk to a product whose whole advantage is avoiding paperwork, and it would take one call to a Spanish energy lawyer to close.

Prior art

The approach here is deliberately not segmentation, and it is worth saying why. A 2025 Bocconi Students for Machine Learning project by Bryan Bisetti, Andrea Duico and Filip Juren trained a U-Net to segment rooftops for PV suitability, training on Ljubljana and testing on Milan. Their reported results are instructive: about 95% training accuracy and 85% IoU, against sub-80% validation accuracy and roughly 50% IoU, failing on sloped roofs. Their own stated next step was to add azimuth and 3D geometry.

That is the reason this model does not start there. A rooftop mask tells you area; it does not tell you tilt, azimuth, or what the building opposite is doing at four o'clock in December — and those three are what the money depends on. So this model takes footprints as given and spends its effort on geometry and economics instead. Where segmentation would earn its place is in generating footprints and obstruction maps at city scale, which is failure mode five above.
