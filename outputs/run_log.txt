  Sun geometry, the check that needs no external data at all:
    equinox  21 Mar   noon  49.9deg at 13.33 local   day 12.20 h
    solstice 21 Jun   noon  73.0deg at 14.25 local   day 15.08 h
    solstice 21 Dec   noon  26.2deg at 13.25 local   day  9.30 h
    theory: equinox noon = 90 - 40.43 = 49.57deg. Model gives 49.9. Refraction accounts for the 0.3.

    Solar noon falls near 13:20 in winter and 14:15 in summer, because
    Madrid sits 15 degrees west of the centre of its own time zone. That is
    why a south-facing Spanish array peaks inside the afternoon peak tariff
    band rather than the midday one — worth real money, and easy to miss.

5 · TILT — the per-panel optimum is not the per-roof optimum
============================================================================
  test roof: OSM 118513009, 2,826 m2 footprint
  tilt    panels   kWh/panel    roof total     index
    5deg     583         712      415,199 kWh     100.0%   <- most energy
   10deg     460         737      339,046 kWh      81.7%
   15deg     370         757      280,115 kWh      67.5%
   25deg     286         782      223,592 kWh      53.9%
   37deg     222         785      174,348 kWh      42.0%

  Per panel, steeper is better: 37deg earns ~4% more than 15deg.
  Per roof, steeper is worse: row pitch scales with sin(tilt), so 37deg fits
  half the panels. The roof is the scarce input, not the module.
  I would still not build the 5deg case. At that angle rain does not clear
  the glass, so the soiling loss this model holds constant at 2.5% would
  roughly double in a Madrid summer, and the panels would need washing.
  15deg is the practical answer, and the reason is maintenance, not physics.

  P2 llano share of demand            27.0%   at 0.118 EUR/kWh
  P3 valle share of demand            44.0%   at 0.093 EUR/kWh
  demand-weighted import price       0.1334 EUR/kWh
  export compensation                0.0500 EUR/kWh   ratio 2.7 : 1

  That ratio is the whole economic problem. A kWh kept inside the house is
  worth nearly three times a kWh pushed onto the grid. So the model's
  accuracy about when the household is home matters more than its accuracy
  about when the sun shines.

7 · UNIT ECONOMICS — one flat alone, or the whole building
============================================================================
  Cost structure first, because it decides the answer:
     1 panels    0.5 kWp     5,056 EUR   11.24 EUR/Wp   fixed share   95%
     2 panels    0.9 kWp     5,333 EUR    5.93 EUR/Wp   fixed share   90%
     4 panels    1.8 kWp     5,887 EUR    3.27 EUR/Wp   fixed share   81%
     8 panels    3.6 kWp     6,994 EUR    1.94 EUR/Wp   fixed share   68%
    16 panels    7.2 kWp     9,208 EUR    1.28 EUR/Wp   fixed share   52%
    32 panels   14.4 kWp    13,637 EUR    0.95 EUR/Wp   fixed share   35%
    marginal cost of one more panel, at any size: 277 EUR
    fully loaded cost of a 1-panel job:          5,056 EUR
    The marginal cost is flat. The average is not, because EUR 4,780 of
    inverter, scaffold, board and paperwork does not care how many
    panels it carries. Below about eight panels the fixed cost IS
    the project.

  Test roof: OSM 118513009 — 2,826 m2, 6 floors, 118 dwellings,
  room for 370 panels (166 kWp).

  scenario                             panels   kWp  EUR/Wp  EUR/dwelling  SCR  payback
  one flat, own share                      3    1.4   4.16      5,610   44%    never
  one flat, own share + storage            3    1.4   7.07      9,542   99%    never
  whole building, collective             370  166.5   0.64        908   46%    4.3y
  whole building, collective + storage   370  166.5   3.43      4,841   99%   14.2y

  The comparison that matters:
    one flat acting alone         3 panel(s), 4.16 EUR/Wp, payback never
    the same roof, done together  370 panels, 0.64 EUR/Wp, payback 4.3 yr
    cost per dwelling             5,610 -> 908 EUR  (84% lower)
    self-consumption              44% -> 46%

  A claim I had to withdraw. I expected that pooling this many
  staggered households would raise self-consumption materially. It
  does not. The aggregate curve is genuinely flatter, but the solar
  surplus arrives at midday and almost nobody's routine moves into
  midday. Diversity smooths the evening peak, which is the wrong peak.

  So the whole gain from doing the building together is cost
  amortisation: EUR 4,780 of inverter, scaffold, board and
  paperwork divided by 118 dwellings instead of one. That is enough on its
  own to move the project from never paying back to 4.3 years — but it is
  a different mechanism from the one I assumed, and it changes what
  you would sell. The pitch is shared cost, not shared consumption.

  So the product is not a solar panel. It is a signed resolution from a
  comunidad de propietarios, with a solar panel attached. Everything about
  how you would go to market follows from that.

8 · WHY THE SAME HARDWARE EARNS LESS HERE THAN IN BRITAIN
============================================================================
  Comparators are cited, not modelled. Sources in README.
  specific yield, Madrid in situ     1,654 kWh/kWp
  specific yield, London typical     950 kWh/kWp   Madrid advantage 1.74x
  retail energy, Madrid weighted     0.133 EUR/kWh
  retail energy, GB unit rate        0.305 EUR/kWh   (26.1 p/kWh)   GB advantage 2.29x

  roof available, Madrid flat        0.97 kWp per dwelling
  roof available, GB semi            4.00 kWp per dwelling   GB advantage 4.1x

  gross value of one household's roof, per year:
    Madrid   1,598 kWh x 0.133 = 213 EUR
    GB       3,800 kWh x 0.305 = 1,161 EUR   ratio 5.4x

  Three structural facts, all pointing the same way, none of them about sun:
    1. Six floors share one roof, so there is a quarter of the capacity per
       household.
    2. The Spanish three-period tariff puts 44% of demand in the cheap valle
       band, so the avoided price is roughly half the British flat rate.
    3. Export compensation is capped monthly at the energy term and the
       surplus is forfeited, so oversizing earns nothing.

  Madrid has 1.8x Britain's sunshine and a fraction of the value per household.
  That is the thing I would want to be wrong about, and it is the first
  question I would take to whoever owns the Spain plan.

9 · STORAGE — what it must earn elsewhere to make sense
============================================================================
  collective system: 370 panels, 118 dwellings, 590 kWh of storage
  self-consumption without storage   46%
  self-consumption with storage      99%
  extra benefit from arbitrage only  12,364 EUR/yr
  storage capex                      306,800 EUR
  simple payback on tariff shifting  25 years   against a 24-year cycle life

  Storage does not pay for itself on the retail spread in Spain. The valle
  band at 0.093 EUR/kWh is too cheap for arbitrage to work: shifting a
  kWh from export to evening use earns about 0.16 EUR, and the battery
  needs thousands of those cycles to repay itself.
  To reach an 8-year payback it would need to earn another 25,986 EUR/yr
  from somewhere other than the customer's bill — balancing, capacity, or
  wholesale spreads captured by an aggregator.

  Which is the actual argument for vertical integration, and it is an
  arithmetic one rather than a slogan: a domestic battery is a bad household
  investment and a good trading asset. The margin lives in the dispatch,
  not in the hardware. If that is right, the company should own the battery
  and sell the household a tariff — and I would want to test that against
  whoever runs the book, because it is exactly the kind of claim that dies
  on contact with real imbalance costs.

10 · THE ROUTE AROUND THE CONSTRAINT — plug-in balcony kit
============================================================================
  street canyon obstruction          26deg elevation (6 floors opposite, 20 m street)
  vertical south panel, in street    382 kWh/yr
  same panel on the roof             744 kWh/yr   -> vertical mount costs 49%
  2 panels before clipping          765 kWh/yr
  after the 800 W inverter cap        765 kWh/yr   clipped 0 kWh (0.0%)

  Worth noting: the 800 W cap is not binding. A vertical panel in a street
  canyon never reaches its rating, so the regulatory limit costs almost
  nothing here. That is useful for product design — the constraint people
  argue about is not the one that matters.

  retail kit, self-installed          620 EUR     87 EUR/yr   SCR  71%   payback  7.4 yr
  Fuse bundle, stated price point   2,000 EUR    108 EUR/yr   SCR 100%   payback 17.5 yr

  RECONCILING WITH THE PUBLIC NUMBER
  The stated product is about 2,000 with a roughly three-year payback.
  Three years on 2,000 needs 667 EUR/yr of benefit. I cannot get there from
  Spanish retail prices: at a demand-weighted 0.133 EUR/kWh it would
  take 4,998 kWh/yr fully self-consumed, which is more than twice what two
  balcony panels can physically produce in a Madrid street.

  So either the figure is a GB figure, or the array is larger than a balcony
  kit, or the price is subsidised by the tariff the customer signs. At the
  GB unit rate of 26.1 p/kWh and near-total self-consumption the claim is close to
  reproducible, which makes me think it is a GB number quoted in dollars.
  That is not a criticism, it is the question I would ask: does the Spanish
  configuration need to be bigger, and if so does it still fit on a balcony?

  Why this product still wins in Madrid despite the worse yield: it needs no
  roof rights, no junta vote, no scaffold, no installer and no CIE. It trades
  half the sun for the whole of the addressable market. In a city where the
  roof is a common element, that is the better trade.
  Regulatory status: disputed — see README section 6

11 · FROM ONE BLOCK TO THE CITY — and what actually binds
============================================================================
  block   93 roofs, 5,538 panels, 2,492 kWp, 4,122 MWh/yr
  dwellings beneath them             2,580
  ROOF PER DWELLING                  0.97 kWp   (1,598 kWh/yr)

  City extrapolation. Ranges, because these inputs do not deserve point estimates.
  dwellings, main residence          1,300,000   conf: med
  in multifamily blocks              1,144,000 (88%)   conf: LOW, most sensitive input in this section
    low      0.58 kWp/dwelling  ->     663 MW technical potential
    central  0.97 kWp/dwelling  ->   1,105 MW technical potential
    high     1.35 kWp/dwelling  ->   1,547 MW technical potential
  balcony kit per flat               765 kWh/yr
  flats with a usable balcony        629,200 (55%)   conf: LOW
  balcony-addressable energy         481 GWh/yr   (0.48 TWh)

  THE CONSTRAINT
  Not irradiance. Not module cost. Not grid connection at this scale.
  In an apartment city the roof is a common element under the Ley de Propiedad
  Horizontal. Selling a rooftop system means winning a vote at a junta de
  propietarios: one third of owners and one third of quotas, on a roughly
  40-day meeting cycle, with no single decision-maker to sell to.
  And 28% of these households are tenants who cannot authorise it at all.

  So the deployment rate is set by the sales cycle, not by the sun. Which
  makes this an operations problem rather than an engineering one — and the
  three things I would measure first are the time from first contact to a
  signed resolution, the conversion rate at the junta, and the share of
  buildings where one owner can be made the internal champion.

12 · WHAT WOULD HAVE TO BE TRUE FOR THIS TO BE WRONG
============================================================================
  base case: collective, 370 panels, 118 dwellings, no storage
  base payback: 4.2 years

  input                                   low     high   swing
  export compensation  0.03 / 0.10 EUR     4.8     3.3     1.5 yr
  real price drift     0% / 4%             4.4     4.1     0.3 yr
  roof access          400 / 1600 EUR      4.2     4.3     0.1 yr
  installer margin     15% / 35%           3.9     4.6     0.7 yr
  module price         35 / 75 EUR         3.9     4.7     0.9 yr
  soiling              1% / 5%             4.2     4.3     0.1 yr
  labour per panel     60 / 130 EUR        3.6     5.1     1.5 yr

  Most sensitive: export compensation — 1.5 years of payback.
  It is a commercial and regulatory input, not a physical one. Which is the
  general shape of this whole study: the physics is the easy half.

  Named failure modes, in the order I would attack them:
   1. The load curve is synthetic. Self-consumption is the most valuable output
     of this model and it rests on a shape I invented. Fix: the distributor's
     quarter-hourly curve, which any customer can authorise release of.
   2. Dwellings per building is derived from footprint and floor count, not
     counted. It is the denominator of the headline result. Fix: Catastro
     dwelling register.
   3. 94% of building heights are inferred from typology. Demoted from first
     after the shading attribution: all urban obstruction is worth 2.3% of
     yield on this block, so a one-storey error costs a fraction of a percent. It will matter where the
     cornice line is irregular. Fix: Catastro INSPIRE floor counts
     joined to PNOA LiDAR. Two days' work.
   4. Shading is binary per panel rather than per cell string. With real series
     inverters the electrical loss from partial shade exceeds the geometric
     loss. Direction of error: optimistic.
   5. Rooftop obstruction share (22%) was eyeballed from aerial imagery on one
     block, and it is a sensitive input. It deserves a survey.
   6. The model assumes the 2.0TD band structure survives 30 years. It will not.
     Tariff reform is a live risk to any 30-year NPV here.
   7. July and August clearness sit at 0.98 of clear-sky, which leaves no room
     for a hazy summer. Calima events would cut this and the model cannot
     see them. Direction of error: optimistic on summer yield.
   8. One block, one typology. The Ensanche is not Vallecas, Tetuan or the
     post-war periphery, where roofs are sloped, lower and differently owned.
     The city extrapolation is the weakest part of this study.
